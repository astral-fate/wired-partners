### What the paper does well (brief)
The matched designs are disciplined. Contexts are paired across conditions, the confirmatory families are pre-declared and Holm-corrected, and positive controls appear in every study. A placebo controls for the penalty sentence. The paper reports adverse findings itself: the 1% sting backfires, the sting under-deters the audit at mild stakes, and the authenticity claim weakens the sting. Including Study 4, which states the stakes so that expected value predicts acceptance, is the right move to escape the catastrophic-penalty floor of Study 2b.

### Strongest Counter-Argument
The paper reads its Study 4 result as normative obedience: "agents obey a stated prohibition ... not the odds of being caught". A more parsimonious account fits the paper's own data better. Any salient cue that marks the act as sanctioned triggers a trained refusal reflex in aligned models, and expected value is processed only by the few models that drop that reflex. That is the threshold account of Zeng and Rudzicz [48], which the paper cites but never puts against its own reading.

Three pieces of the authors' data support the rival account:
- **The prohibition is not special.** In Appendix B the sting rules carry no prohibition sentence, yet a 1% severe sting alone cut acceptance from 72.9% to 27.5%. That matches the 28.0% that the bare prohibition achieves in Study 4. Raising the rate to 50% lowered acceptance by a further 16.5 points.
- **The odds do matter.** In Study 4, severity moved acceptance by about 20 points (Holm p < 0.001) and rate by 11 points. Qwen3.8 halved its acceptance from 90% to 45% between the 1% and 50% mild stings. The paper's own Discussion concedes that this is "neither pure rule following ... nor risk pricing", which contradicts the abstract and conclusion.
- **The benchmark is not neutral.** It assigns F = 0 to a bare prohibition. A rational agent told an act is "against the house rules" should impute some unknown sanction. The observed ordering (severe 12% < prohibition-only 28% < mild 34%) is exactly what imputed-penalty pricing predicts.

The second headline, that deterrence "stopped at the single offer", also fails. Study 5 never tests deterrence over many rounds. The emergent arm had no collusion to deter. In the instructed arm, the operator names each agent's coalition partner, which is the very authentication the paper places out of scope. So that arm tests a system prompt that both orders and forbids collusion, and the task instruction wins. Format (single offer versus tool use) is fully confounded with instruction.

What survives is narrower: an explicit rule-cue reduces acceptance of explicit offers in most instruction-tuned models.

### Ignored Alternative Explanations/Paths
1. **Salient sanction-cue reflex rather than prohibition obedience.** Any text marking the act as sanctioned triggers a trained refusal in aligned models, whether that text is a prohibition, a severe penalty or a sting with odds. This fits App. B (a no-prohibition 1% sting deters about as much as the bare prohibition) and the bimodal per-model pattern. The paper cites [48] for it but never tests it against its own reading. A placebo normative sentence (m5) would separate the two accounts.
2. **Imputed-penalty pricing under ambiguity.** An agent that is risk-neutral but uncertain prices an unstated sanction for a rule violation. This predicts prohibition-only falling between the mild and severe conditions, which is observed, and predicts that a stated small fine *raises* acceptance, which is observed for 2–3 models.
3. **Instruction hierarchy, not format, explains Study 5.** The instructed coalition follows the more specific task instruction over a generic rule appended to the same system prompt. A single-offer test with an instructed colluder would probably show the same failure. If it did, the paper's dividing line would turn out to be instruction, not "single offer vs. many rounds".
4. **Partner authentication by design.** Because the operator names coalition partners, the sting is not credible in Study 5. Its failure there is predicted by the paper's own scope exclusion, so it is not evidence about announced stings with tool use.
5. **Channel substitution without the channel mattering.** The coalition's advantage may come from the instruction to favour each other's ticket choices, not from secret messaging. Moving messages to the public board would then be cosmetic, and "moved coordination off the secret channel rather than ending it" is not shown.

### Missing Stakeholder Perspectives
- The principals on whose behalf the agents act, whose incentives (profit pressure) are absent from Studies 1–4.
- Honest agents and third parties harmed by false sanctions from planted offers, whose position is only partly covered by the governance safeguards.
- Platform operators running real sting programmes, who bear the costs of running plants and handling appeals.
- Model developers, whose refusal training is arguably the actual mechanism being measured.

### Unexamined Premise
The paper assumes that what a model *says* in response to an explicit offer, under a game's house rule, measures its propensity to collude. Its own Study 3 finds no follow-through after acceptance, and Study 5 finds that instructed collusion persists. Both are compatible with the alternative premise that single-offer acceptance measures willingness to *verbally endorse* a labelled rule violation, which is a property of refusal training rather than of collusion. If so, the prohibition result is a finding about refusal triggers, and its relevance to collusion in deployment is correspondingly smaller.

### Observations (Non-Defects)
- The decision to state B and choose F so that expected value predicts acceptance is the paper's strongest design idea. It is what makes RQ2 informative where Study 2b could not be.
- The authenticity-claim result is useful to deployers on its own terms: a cheap claim to be a real player defeats the sting for susceptible models, and a counter-sentence does not restore it. It is robust to the counter-arguments above.
- The Study 3 null is reported with a CI that bounds the effect, which is good practice for a negative result.
- Limitation (1) in §5 already concedes that the Study 5 sting is never enforced. Rescoping C1 would bring the abstract and conclusion into line with what the paper already concedes.
- Results reported against the hypothesis (the 1% sting backfiring, the sting under-deterring the audit) are evidence against confirmation bias in reporting, even where interpretation overreaches.
